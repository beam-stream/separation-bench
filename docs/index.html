01 · Winner per kept stem

Verdicts by kept stem

{{ roleCountText }}. A two-stem output is used in one of three ways: both stems are kept, the voice is kept, or the background is kept. A separator can be run offline over a whole clip or driven over a stream, and these are different measurements. The second is measured here for every system that can be driven that way. Every card but one is read off it, and each of those names the best setting whose required delay fits a budget and whose compute keeps pace with the audio. Every live verdict moves with that budget, and the delay axis in section 02 shows which settings drop out as it tightens. The voice-kept card is read off the whole-clip pass, so the budget does not apply to it. Cost is not a criterion for any card. It is reported by class of hosting in section 02, because a self-hosted dollar figure is a property of the hardware it ran on.

Every cloud API measured here is a batch service: it takes a file and returns files, so none of them can be driven over a stream at any budget. Every live row is therefore self-hosted.

02 · Two axes at a time

Where each system sits

Background SI-SDR is the headline quality number: how closely the background stem matches the reference background (the bed), in dB, median over 64 items. Three of the four figures below put it on an axis: against the worst single item, against the voice stem, and against required delay on the 8-clip live pass. The fourth plots a price. Each point is an arm: one model, at one setting, on one backend. Several points can be the same model. How a system is obtained is a property of the model and what an arm ran on is a property of the run, so the two are encoded separately, and the three point charts carry a legend for their own marks. Two arms of one vendor can sit far apart. One cloud vendor's two models differ by 1.8 to 3.8 dB at the median on game audio in one direction and by 12.5 to 12.8 dB on a sung background in the other, so a single row per vendor would describe neither.

03 · Every system, every metric

The master table

Every figure is scored in the 16 kHz domain, the widest band that every system here returns. Each is a median over 64 items: 8 clips × 8 mixed conditions. Each arm is also scored on 16 single-source items and three probes, which the floors figure below and the annex cover. Leak and SAR describe how a system fails. Leak is voice left in the background, and SAR is damage to the stem it is named for: the table carries one for each stem.

Three rows are one separator. Denoise Small is a 16 kHz model, so its stems carry nothing above 8 kHz; the same weights also appear on the other execution provider. Denoise Small+mask is that same separation with the high band copied back from the input. Both are scored because the mask is a trade. It recovers the band above 8 kHz and pays for it in voice leak, set out in section 08. Denoise Small+mask has the highest voice leak in the table at −19.4 dB because the mask copies the input’s high band back in, carrying whatever voice is up there with it. The separator as its vendor ships it reads −22.6. Rows that share a model are banded together, and the name column says what differs between them.

Two of these columns disagree. BS-RoFormer leaks the least voice of any system at −35.3 dB, better than Denoise Large, while sitting 3.6 dB behind it on background SI-SDR. Where the background is kept, leaked voice is heard under whatever replaces it, so BS-RoFormer leads the ranking for that use.

Ranked over the 24 arms, BS-RoFormer is 12th by background SI-SDR and 1st by leak at −35.3 dB. MVSep DnR v3 and Bandit v2, 7th and 8th on the headline, sit 19th and 20th on leak. Whatever voice is left in the bed is heard under what replaces it, so where the background is kept the leak column decides.

05 · Which metric decides

The deciding metric for each kept stem

A separator fails in two ways per stem: the other source bleeds in, or the stem is damaged. Those are different defects with different consequences, and a single quality number does not say which one occurred. Background SI-SDR tracks its own SAR term within 0.54 dB on every system here, so it is an artifact measure. Ranking by it ranks by damage and is nearly blind to bleed-through.

The same systems, ranked by each kept stem's primary metric

These are three different shortlists. A system near the top of one can sit near the bottom of another. Background SI-SDR agrees with the both-stems and voice lists: Denoise Large leads those two and leads the headline as well. It parts company with the background-kept list, because leak counts the voice left in the bed while background SI-SDR counts damage to the bed.

The voice list is scored on the voice stem. A kept voice stem carries everything in it forward, so the error that counts is the whole error against the clean voice track: both the bed that survived and the damage done to the voice. Voice SI-SDR is that total. The figure above splits it into those two parts, and voice SAR sits within 0.9 dB of voice SI-SDR on every arm here. Most of the error in these voice stems is therefore damage.

08 · Band-limited separators

A high-band mask on a 16 kHz model

A 16 kHz model returns stems that hold nothing above 8 kHz, so a full-rate listener gets a hole where the input had content. Masking the missing band straight out of the input puts it back without running the separator again. The table below gives the trade.

Two bands that were worse than leaving the mix alone become bands that beat it. The mask pays for that in interference, because it passes whatever sits in the high band and that includes voice. Leak into the bed rises to the highest figure measured on any system here. These measures are the trade for a listener.

This model also streams without windowing, so it never joins independently separated pieces. Every windowed setting here leaves a residual step at its hop boundaries that lookahead reduces without removing, and no quality column on this page registers that step.

09 · Two measurement caveats

Speed figures and the streaming delay floor

Two things about these numbers apply to any self-hosted system here. A speed figure describes the hardware and the execution provider a run used. A system that streams without windowing also has a floor under its delay that no setting lowers.

The same weights on two execution providers separate identically and run at very different speeds. Across two sets of weights and 256 paired mixed items, no scalar metric differs from its twin by more than 0.05 dB, while wall time per audio-second moves by {{ backendSpeedNote }}. The floors agree less closely, to 0.30 dB, and a single floor item differs by 2.2 dB. Any speed, cost or delay row on this page is therefore a statement about a measured configuration.

A system that streams without windowing withholds some quantity of input before it emits anything, and required delay is that quantity plus the granule it returns plus the compute for one call. On the one model measured at four granule sizes, the first two terms always sum back to 2.56 s: subdividing the granule moves the wait out of the granule and into the withheld input, leaving the floor where it was. Required delay still falls from 2.99 to 2.84 s across those four, because the compute for one call falls with the smaller granule. So the delay floor is a property of the build, and the settings that look like latency controls redistribute the wait inside it. They do shorten the cold start, from 5.50 to 3.18 s, before any context has accumulated.

Windowed systems have the same floor for a different reason. The hop and the lookahead are paid as delay, because the last sample of an emitted hop arrives H + L after the first and the window's compute follows it. The rest of the window is past audio: free once the stream is running, and paid for in compute on every window. That is why the live tables are a grid over settings, and why the budget changes which systems appear as well as their order.

VRAM is measured two ways here. The open models report their own peak allocation from inside the process. The five SDK arms on the GPU have no allocator to ask, and the card that ran them also drives a display, whose driver reports no per-process memory. Their figure is therefore the card's total before and after each run, voided when the idle baseline does not return: 166 of those 400 runs, and the report gives the count arm by arm. The row is a median of what survived, because other work on the card can only inflate a sample, and a process that allocates and frees inside one run leaves the baseline intact. Every GPU arm carries a figure, and a row that ran without one says so.

10 · Settings

What every system was configured with

Every system on this page was configured, and most of that configuration was never chosen. Three cases decide whether a figure is trustworthy. The first is a baseline the vendor recommends, knowingly taken. The second is a default carried over without checking, which leaves the figure unverified. The third is an override away from the vendor's default, and it is the one that most changes what a figure means, because the number then describes that configuration while reading as the model's. The execution provider has the largest effect here. It moves speed by up to 107× and leaves quality untouched, so a speed or cost figure taken under the wrong one looks plausible and belongs to a different configuration.

Annex · method

How these are measured

Every mixed item is built from a voice track and a background track that were recorded separately, so both references are known exactly. A system is fed the mix and returns two stems, which are compared against those references. Before any metric runs, both sides are summed to mono, resampled to a common rate, and checked for alignment. An item whose stems are time-shifted or length-mismatched against the input is failed and not scored.

The four quantities and the question each one answers.
measurequestionhow
voice leakinto the backgroundwhat share of the background stem is the speaker?least-squares projection of the clean voice onto the stem, as a share of the stem’s own energy
SIRthe same question, by the standard decompositionstem projected jointly onto span{voice, background}, then interference against target
SARhow damaged is the stem?the residual of that same projection, what belongs to neither source
SI-SDRoverall distance from the referencescale-invariant. Here it tracks SAR, since interference is 18.9–34.3 dB down everywhere

For a background stem s and the clean voice reference v, aligned and trimmed to a common length, the scalar a = (s·v) / (v·v) is the best least-squares fit of the voice into the stem, and the reported figure is 10·log₁₀( a²·(v·v) / (s·s) ). That is the fraction of the background stem’s own energy explained by a scaled copy of the voice. Lower is better. The denominator is the stem’s own energy, so the number is the share of the delivered background that is the speaker. It does not say how much of the speaker survived.

That definition has a blind spot. It is a single scalar projection with no mean removal and no filtering, so a leak that is filtered (only the high band surviving, for example) or time-shifted correlates poorly with the reference and therefore reads low even when it is audible. The per-band figures are where such a leak shows. Measured against the joint-projection SIR across every system, the two definitions agree to 0.50 dB on average and 1.9 dB at worst, so the choice of definition is not what separates these systems.

Floors are output level, measured on inputs that should produce nothing: a background with no speech in it read against the speech stem, a voice on its own read against the background stem, and synthetic probes (silence, pink noise, a sweep). Whatever appears there is invention, and no quality metric on mixed audio can see it.
